# Pitch scripts (English)

## 30-second pitch
Every monsoon the Jamuna eats farmland and homes. Radar satellites can map that loss through clouds — but a quick post-monsoon map has to rely on only two or three comparable Sentinel-1 scenes. We asked: how many scenes are enough, and can simple safeguards — requiring several scenes to agree, and refusing to decide when a pixel is too close to the threshold — make sparse maps more reliable? We tested this on a 12-km Kurigram reach against an independent Sentinel-2 reference. Two to three scenes already gave stable maps. Our agreement-plus-abstention rule made the decided pixels very reliable and ignored a bad scene that fooled the baseline — but it left about one-eighth of the bank strip undecided, and we report that cost openly.

## Two-minute presentation
**(Problem — 20 s)** The Jamuna erodes hundreds of hectares every year; CEGIS monitored about 348 hectares in 2025. Sentinel-1 radar can see through clouds, and Freihardt and Frey showed in 2023 that a simple threshold rule in Google Earth Engine can map past erosion, even one month after the monsoon.

**(Gap — 20 s)** But a one-month map over our reach uses only two or three scenes from the same orbit — not because of clouds, but because each orbit passes only every 12 days. Freihardt and Frey studied window length through backscatter statistics and validated land cover, not the erosion map itself. Nobody we found measured how erosion-map accuracy changes with the number of scenes.

**(Method — 35 s)** We reproduced their rule — VV, ascending orbit, circular 7-pixel filter, pre-monsoon composite above a threshold and post-monsoon below it. Then we added two safeguards. A — agreement: a pixel is eroded only if at least m of k scenes say sand or water. G — gating: if a value is within δ dB of the threshold we label it UNCERTAIN and always report how many pixels were decided. Our reference is Sentinel-2: land that was vegetated before and is sand or water after, connected to the channel, on the outer banks — an independent optical sensor, not field truth. All thresholds, m and δ were tuned on the 2024 monsoon and frozen before testing on 2025. We evaluated every possible combination of 1, 2, 3, 6 and 8 scenes, plus the maps one could actually have made on 8 November, 20 November and 2 December.

**(Results — 35 s)** With the published rule, going from one scene to three raised mean F1 from 0.70 to 0.77 and cut false erosion from 22 hectares to 2; more scenes added almost nothing. Single scenes were risky: depending on the date, F1 ranged from 0.30 to 0.77. One late-January scene showed field-shaped dark patches and produced nearly 200 hectares of false erosion on its own — SAGE-River's agreement rule kept such two-scene maps at about one hectare. On the pixels it decides, SAGE-River reached F1 0.86 to 0.90 with almost no false erosion. But it abstained on 9 to 13 percent of the bank strip, which contained about a quarter to a third of the real erosion. Counted as 'no erosion', it was not consistently better than a fairly tuned baseline. So our answer is: two to three scenes are enough for a usable map, and agreement plus abstention buys reliability, not more detections.

**(Honesty & impact — 10 s)** This is one reach and one test year, with an optical reference, and no forecasting. But the protocol — check scene count, require agreement, abstain and report coverage — is cheap and could flag when a quick post-monsoon map should not be trusted.
